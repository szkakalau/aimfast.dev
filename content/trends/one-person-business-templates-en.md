## What is it

One-Person Business Templates are pre-built frameworks, boilerplates, and case-study blueprints that package the operational DNA of a solo-founded software company into a repeatable, purchasable format. Think of them as the "recipe cards" for the indie hacker economy — a GitHub repository containing your auth, billing, and landing page, plus a Notion doc explaining how to acquire the first 100 customers and a spreadsheet modeling when you hit ramen profitability.

The technical essence is modularity: rather than hiring a dev agency or spending 6 months learning Stripe integration, a founder buys a starter kit that compresses the setup phase from months to days. The business significance is bigger: these templates commoditize the boring 80% of SaaS creation — authentication, payments, CRM scaffolding — so a solo developer can focus on the 20% that creates differentiation: the niche workflow, the specialized algorithm, the unique distribution angle.

This is not a product category in the traditional sense. It is an *infrastructure layer* for the one-person economy. The buyer is not a company with a procurement department; the buyer is an individual developer with a credit card and a weekend. That changes everything about pricing, support expectations, and go-to-market motion.

## Why now

Three forces converge in mid-2026 to make one-person business templates explosive. First, AI coding assistants have collapsed the marginal cost of writing boilerplate code. A developer who used to need a week to scaffold a Stripe-connected Next.js app can now do it in an afternoon with Cursor or Copilot. That means the *value* of a template is no longer the code itself — it is the accumulated business judgment embedded in the template: which pricing page converts, which onboarding email sequence retains, which feature set to launch with.

Second, the "layoff-to-launch" pipeline is at peak volume. The 2024–2025 tech contraction created a cohort of senior engineers with severance packages, domain expertise, and a desperate need for income. These people have skills but no playbook. They are the perfect buyers for a $99 template that tells them exactly what to build and how to sell it.

Third, the platform economics have matured. Stripe now supports 135+ currencies, GitHub Sponsors is normalized, and global payment rails make it trivially easy for a solo founder in Lagos to sell to a customer in Oslo. The infrastructure was not ready for this in 2022. It is ready now. The growth rate of 100% in mentions reflects this timing — the conversation is moving from "is this possible?" to "give me the template."

That has not happened yet at scale.

## Who's Behind It

The current "whales" are not corporations — they are individual developers who accidentally became micro-brands. The most visible names in this space include Marc Lou (who sells shipfast and other boilerplates), Tony Dinh (TypingMind, Blackbox AI), and the cohort of developers who publish "Open Source Alternative to X" repos that double as lead magnets for their paid templates.

On the community side, the drivers are the Indie Hackers forum, the r/SideProject subreddit, and the Chinese-language w2solo community (which has been ahead of the West on solo-company playbooks). These communities serve as both distribution channels and social proof engines. A template that gets 200 upvotes on Reddit with a "built this in 3 days, made $2k" title is worth more than a $5,000 ad campaign.

The competitive dynamic is fragmented. There is no dominant player, no category leader, no recognized "standard" for what a one-person business template should contain. This is the classic early-market condition where the winner is the person who defines the category, not the person with the best code. The window is open precisely because nobody has claimed it yet.

## TAM & Market Size

The addressable market is the global population of developers who want to start a side business but lack the playbook. Using conservative numbers: GitHub has 100+ million developers. Industry surveys consistently show 30–40% want to start a side project that generates revenue. That is 30–40 million potential buyers, but the realistic serviceable market is much smaller — perhaps 1–2 million who will actually pay money in the next 12 months.

The buyer profile is specific: a developer aged 25–40, employed or recently laid off, with 3+ years of coding experience, who has tried and failed at a side project before. They are not looking for code — they can write code. They are looking for *certainty*. They want to know which problem to solve, how to price it, and how to get the first 50 customers.

Price tolerance is the good news. This buyer is used to paying for developer tools — they spend $20/month on ChatGPT, $15/month on GitHub Copilot, and $10/month on Spotify. A one-time $99–$149 purchase for a template that saves them 2 months of fumbling is an easy yes. The demand score of 40/100 reflects that the *intent* is there but the *product* has not yet been delivered at scale. Whoever ships the first truly comprehensive, well-supported template will see demand spike.

## Competitive Landscape

The existing players fall into three tiers. Tier one is the boilerplate-as-product companies: ShipFast ($199 one-time), Supastarter ($149), and Divjoy ($49). These sell code scaffolding — auth, payments, UI components — but they stop at the technical layer. They do not tell you what to *build* or how to *sell* it. Tier two is the course creators: levelsio (Pieter Levels) sells his Twitter playbook, and various "make $10k/month" courses sell for $500–$2,000. These cover strategy but not implementation. Tier three is the free content: the case studies and GitHub repos that are inspiring but unstructured.

The gap is obvious: there is no product that combines the *code* of tier one with the *business playbook* of tier two at a price point below $200. That is your entry point.

Big Tech is unlikely to enter this space — it is too small, too fragmented, and too "unsexy" for a company like Microsoft or Google to care. You have 12–18 months before a well-funded competitor emerges, and even then, the market is big enough for multiple players. The competition score of 20/100 is accurate: you are not fighting incumbents; you are fighting obscurity.

## Business Model

The recommended model is a tiered one-time purchase with a premium newsletter upsell. Do not do subscription for the template itself — this buyer is skeptical of recurring costs and will compare you unfavorably to the $199 one-time competitors. Instead, structure it as:

- **Starter Template** ($79 one-time): Code boilerplate + basic README + 1 niche vertical (e.g., "SaaS for freelancers").
- **Complete Playbook** ($199 one-time): Starter + 50-page business playbook (pricing, positioning, first-100-customers plan) + 10 video walkthroughs + private Discord access.
- **Monthly Newsletter** ($9/month): New case study every week, updated playbook sections, template updates.

The 12-month revenue forecast assumes you launch the Starter at month 1, the Playbook at month 2, and the newsletter at month 3:

- **Conservative**: 200 Starter sales + 50 Playbook sales + 100 newsletter subs = $15,800 + $9,950 + $9,000 = **$34,750**
- **Base**: 500 Starter + 150 Playbook + 300 newsletter = $39,500 + $29,850 + $27,000 = **$96,350**
- **Optimistic**: 1,200 Starter + 400 Playbook + 800 newsletter = $94,800 + $79,600 + $72,000 = **$246,400**

CAC is low because distribution is organic — Reddit, Indie Hackers, and SEO. Budget $0 on ads for the first 6 months. If you spend 20 hours per week on content and community engagement, your "cost" is opportunity cost only. Payback period is immediate: a $199 Playbook sale costs you nothing but the 30 minutes it takes to answer a Discord question.

## MVP Blueprint

Your MVP can ship in 5 days, not 14. The key is ruthless scope-cutting. Here is the spec:

**Day 1–2: Code Boilerplate.** A Next.js 14 + Tailwind + Stripe + Supabase template with a landing page, auth, and a single subscription tier. Do NOT build an admin dashboard, do NOT add multi-tenant support, do NOT write tests. This is the "dumbest possible" version that still works. Use create-t3-app as your base and modify.

**Day 3: Business Playbook PDF.** Write a 20-page document covering: how to choose a niche, 3 pricing strategies with real examples, the exact cold-email/DM script for first 10 customers, and a 30-day launch calendar. Use ChatGPT to draft, then rewrite 50% in your own voice. The playbook is the differentiator — the code is table stakes.

**Day 4: Landing Page + Payment Flow.** Use the boilerplate itself to sell the boilerplate (dogfooding). Set up Gumroad or Lemon Squeezy for payments — do not build custom checkout. This saves 2 days.

**Day 5: Launch Package.** Write 5 Reddit posts (different subreddits), 3 Indie Hackers posts, and 1 Twitter/X thread. Schedule them. Create a simple Discord server for buyers. Ship.

**Tech stack decision:** Next.js + Tailwind + Supabase + Stripe + Lemon Squeezy. Do not use Django, do not use AWS, do not use anything that requires a DevOps background. The MVP is not about elegance; it is about shipping before the competition notices.

## Risk Assessment

**Risk 1: The market is talk, not action.** The 10 mentions with 100% growth could be a bubble of aspiration. People *discuss* one-person businesses more than they *buy* templates for them. Validation: before building the full product, launch a $10 "pre-order" landing page and measure conversion. If fewer than 5% of visitors pre-order, the demand is not real.

**Risk 2: AI makes templates obsolete.** If AI coding assistants get good enough to generate a complete business-in-a-box from a single prompt, the template market collapses. This is a 12–24 month risk. Mitigation: pivot from selling *code* to selling *judgment* — the playbook, the case studies, the community. Code can be generated; experience cannot.

**Risk 3: The "whale" problem.** A well-funded player (e.g., a company like Framer or Webflow) could add a "one-person business template" feature to their existing product and crush you on distribution. Mitigation: move fast, build a community, and own a niche (e.g., "templates for solo fintech founders") that is too small for a big company to care about.

**Walk-away trigger:** If you spend 4 weeks, publish 10 pieces of content, and get fewer than 50 email signups, the demand is not strong enough. Walk away and apply the same playbook to a different niche.

## Related Terms

**Micro-SaaS Communities** — The broader movement of developers building small, focused SaaS products. One-Person Business Templates are the *enabler* for this trend; expect them to grow together as template buyers become micro-SaaS founders.

**AI-Assisted Development** — The tooling that makes solo development feasible. As AI coding assistants improve, the barrier to entry drops, which increases demand for the *business* knowledge that templates provide.
